    How to use the ladder

    1. Write one line from your piece. It can be a small detail or a big idea.
    2. Press So what? to climb. Write what the line below means, or what it's an example of.
    3. Press Like what? to step down. Write something a reader could see, hear, or touch that shows the line above.
    4. Read your rungs as a passage. Strike the middle rungs and see how a high line reads right next to a low one.

    Why move up and down

    A reader needs both ends. Details alone are a pile of objects with no reason to care about them. Ideas alone are claims with nothing to picture. Most pieces that land move between the two: a mug, then what the mug means, then the next thing she does with it.

    Hayakawa called it "dead-level abstracting" when a writer stays on one rung for too long. Climbing the ladder line by line is a quick way to notice where you've been standing, and to find the rung you're missing.

    Where the ladder comes from

    The abstraction ladder is S. I. Hayakawa's. He introduced it in Language in Action and carried it into Language in Thought and Action (1949), a book about how words shape what we think we know. Hayakawa adapted the idea from Alfred Korzybski's structural differential, a teaching diagram from general semantics. Since then, journalism and writing teachers have adapted it for the page, among them Jack Hart in Storycraft. This tool is our own take on the idea; if it's useful to you, Hayakawa's book is well worth reading.

    Questions

    What is the ladder of abstraction?

    It's a way of picturing language by how specific it is. At the bottom are things you could point at: a chipped mug, a Tuesday, a smell of diesel. At the top are ideas you can only describe: grief, loyalty, progress. Each step up keeps what things have in common and leaves out the particulars. The semanticist S. I. Hayakawa made the ladder famous in Language in Thought and Action.

    Who came up with it?

    S. I. Hayakawa drew the abstraction ladder in Language in Action and kept it in the expanded Language in Thought and Action (1949). He adapted it from the structural differential, a diagram by Alfred Korzybski, the founder of general semantics. Writing teachers and editors have used versions of it ever since.

    Which end of the ladder should I start from?

    Either. If you have a detail you love but don't know why it matters, start there and ask So what? If you have an idea that feels flat, start there and ask Like what? until you reach something a reader could see.

    How many rungs should a ladder have?

    Three to five is plenty for most ideas. The point isn't a tall ladder but a long reach: one rung near the top and one near the bottom. The tool holds up to twelve.
